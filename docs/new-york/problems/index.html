What went wrong?

Where Article 78 cases often go wrong.

First, write it down.

Put every demand, refusal, and objection in writing, with the date and proof of receipt.

  1. The clerk would not take your papers.

    A clerk must accept a petition that meets the written rules. Some clerks turn petitions away for reasons the rules do not list. With no case on file, there is no record to show a judge what went wrong.

    • CPLR §304 · 22 NYCRR §1250.4
    • Brusco v. Braun, 84 N.Y.2d 674 (1994)
    • Klostermann v. Cuomo, 61 N.Y.2d 525, 540–41 (1984)
    • Matter of Council of City of N.Y. v. Bloomberg, 6 N.Y.3d 380 (2006)

  2. Your paper was rejected over its sworn statement.

    If the sworn statement on a paper has a flaw, the law says the other side must object fast and name the flaw. Clerks and court staff sometimes reject the paper on their own. Then there is no objection on file for you to appeal.

    • CPLR §7804(d)
    • CPLR §3022
    • Matter of Miller v. Bd. of Assessors, 91 N.Y.2d 82 (1997)
    • Lentlie v. Egan, 94 A.D.2d 839 (3d Dep't 1983), aff'd 61 N.Y.2d 874 (1984)

  3. Your case against a judge was sent to the wrong court.

    A case against a Supreme Court or County Court judge must start in the Appellate Division. Clerks sometimes send it back to Supreme Court, which is the wrong place. That moves your filing date, and the four-month limit is counted from it.

    • CPLR §506(b)(1)
    • CPLR §7804(b)
    • Nolan v. Lungen, 61 N.Y.2d 788 (1984)

  4. Your case was thrown out over how you served the papers.

    Article 78 has its own rule for giving papers to the other side: at least twenty days before the court date. Courts sometimes use rules meant for other kinds of cases. A case can be thrown out even though the Article 78 rule was met.

    • CPLR §7804(c)
    • CPLR §306-b
    • Matter of Common Council of Albany v. Town Bd. of Bethlehem, 144 A.D.2d 215 (3d Dep't 1988)
    • Matter of Vetrone v. Mackin, 50 A.D.3d 1589 (4th Dep't 2008)

  5. The court decided without a hearing.

    When the two sides disagree about a fact that matters, the law says the court must hold a hearing right away. Courts sometimes decide on the papers alone and skip the hearing. Later courts then treat the facts as if they were decided against you.

    • CPLR §7804(h)
    • CPLR §409(b)
    • Matter of Kickertz v. New York Univ., 25 N.Y.3d 942 (2015)

  6. The court asked whether the refusal made sense.

    When an official has no choice but to act, the court should order the act. It should not ask whether the refusal made sense. Courts sometimes use that softer test anyway and deny the order.

    • CPLR §7803(1) · §7803(3)
    • Klostermann v. Cuomo, 61 N.Y.2d 525 (1984)
    • Brusco v. Braun, 84 N.Y.2d 674 (1994)
    • Matter of Council of City of N.Y. v. Bloomberg, 6 N.Y.3d 380 (2006)
    • Matter of Pell v. Bd. of Educ., 34 N.Y.2d 222 (1974)

  7. Your case was thrown out for a missing record.

    The agency or official you are suing must file the full record of what happened. You do not have to. Courts sometimes throw a case out for a missing record that only the other side can supply.

    • CPLR §7804(e)
    • Matter of Smith v. Town of Plattekill, 169 A.D.3d 1265 (3d Dep't 2019)
    • Matter of Kaur v. New York State Urban Dev. Corp., 15 N.Y.3d 235 (2010)

Void orders.

A void order is one the court had no power to make. It has no legal force, and you can attack it at any time. The usual first step is a motion in the court that made the order (CPLR §5015(a)). If a court keeps acting without power, Article 78 prohibition can stop it (§7803(2)).

Glossary.

Appellate Division
The appeals court above Supreme Court. It sits in four departments.
CPLR
Civil Practice Law and Rules, the New York statute governing civil procedure.
Prohibition
A court order that tells a court or official to stop acting without power.
Record
The papers and testimony the agency or court had in front of it when it decided.
Supreme Court
The main trial court. It is not the highest court.
Void
Without legal force from the start.
